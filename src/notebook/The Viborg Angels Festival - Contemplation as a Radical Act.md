_“The age of spectacle is dead.”_

If my fuzzy memory of the conversation serves me right, that is what I pronounced, late one August night this year, in a hotel bar in Viborg, Denmark, to a companion who had every reason to doubt it. I had drunk one or two Negronis more than a man ought to before tackling anything (other than, perhaps, another Negroni) with such insouciance. Fed up with an age of visual hyperbole, AI imagery, and over-stimulation, I desperately wanted my drunken proclamation to be true. Ever since, I have been trying to work out how much I truly believe it is. In a little over a fortnight I shall, perhaps, begin to find out, because on 27 November the Angels of Viborg festival opens to the public. It is a festival that has been built, largely at my urging, on the proposition that a winter festival can ask its audience not to look, but to contemplate.

This is a note written before the event. If the verdict is broadly positive, I would like this to stand as a record that we weren't sure. If it doesn't work, I would like there to be a record of what we were attempting, and why it was worth trying.

Viborg is a small cathedral city in the middle of Jutland and, like York, a UNESCO City of Media Arts. In the early years of the last century Joakim Skovgaard covered the inside of its cathedral with biblical scenes peopled with the faces of his neighbours, and the city has held its angels close to its heart ever since. Last year it held a small but successful inaugural festival in their name, and it began to wonder how that festival might grow.

The understandable instinct, when a night-time festival wants to grow, is to look to Lyon, Eindhoven and Copenhagen for precedents, and to invite internationally renowned artists to light up the buildings and draw a crowd of spectators, phones held aloft. Over those Negronis, I argued that Viborg cannot win that game and shouldn't play it. At Christmas the town lights itself almost entirely in white, so that rather than shouting for attention, the streets feel tranquil, and the enchantment of the city's understated architecture already does much of the work to set the right tone. Why not be the one city in Europe people travel to in winter, not to consume but to contemplate, to be quiet? Bravely, Lars and his colleagues agreed that sounded like a good idea. Which is how somebody with no direct qualification to curate a Danish festival of angels found himself doing just that.

It is worth being precise about what “contemplate” means in this context. It definitely doesn't mean that nothing happens.

1. **It's one walk, not a set of unrelated attractions.** The route begins and ends at a fountain that we have transformed into a symbolic campfire, and passes through seven 'angel stations' in the gardens and the cobbled streets around the cathedral.
2. **Each station is made of similar things.** A steel cylinder that emits light; benches arranged differently in each station, so that you are repeatedly asked to sit in a different relation to the strangers around you; a short spoken passage looped with music, ending in a question that the angel represented by that place puts to you.
3. **The bright things that demand to be photographed live in the streets between.** The stations themselves offer very little to photograph. They are not there to try to engage you as a spectator. They are trying to hold you, for six or seven minutes, on a cold bench, in a Danish winter.

The Danes and I wrote a manifesto together in York in May. It has a line that has held ever since: the test is not whether you touched it, but whether it touched you.

This is not simply a matter of taste, and here I should own up to the tub I habitually thump. These days, any one of us can conjure an astonishing image on a phone in no time. But as astonishing pictures get cheaper, it gets harder to ask people to stand in the cold to look at a very large one on the side of a building. What the machine cannot do on your behalf is sit beside someone you don't know, in the dark, in December, and be left alone with a question. Render unto the machine the things that are the machine's. That, I think, is what I meant in the bar, and sober I would put it more carefully: spectacle is not dead, but its value seems to me to be declining, and the value of attention and of company is moving upwards in the other direction. 

I have come to think of all this as urgent rather than simply worthwhile. Our culture has been quietly relieving each and every one of us of the need to imagine. Pictures we might once have made in our own heads can now be made for us, instantly. A faculty that is never called upon will, I fear, swiftly atrophy. I think the inner imagination, that private and unrepeatable theatre each of us carries about, is the most human thing we possess; and that to re-engage with it and learn to trust it, in a body, in a place, in the company of strangers, with something actually being asked of us, may be the most important thing that art can do right now. Viborg has already shown me what happens when you ask. When _People We Love_, which I first made in 2020, came to the city in 2023, there was nothing to look at but faces looking at someone they loved, and the audience supplied everything else. So yes, the festival is a risk. But it is a brave one, and I’m proud that Viborg is prepared to take it.

And yet, Lars, who I consider a good friend, and who has a central role in how Viborg presents itself to the world, has been the idea's most searching critic. He greeted our plan with the best line of the summer: this, he said, is what happens when you let five atheists work on angels unsupervised. The accused, and I can vouch for nobody's theology but my own here, were Henrik Zacho, Signe and Martin Klok, Martina Scarpelli and me. Henriette Svenstrup Jensen was in the room too corralling us all, and since not one bench or cylinder on that route would exist without all of them (particularly without the extraordinary work of KLOK visuals), the heresy is properly shared and so, I hope, is whatever credit is going. Lars's fear was that our approach was too slim, too Scandinavian, too underwhelming, and I would be a fool to pretend he might not be right. A family arriving for a Christmas outing with chilly children has been promised a festival, and a festival that asks something of you is a festival some people will understandably decline. I know, all too well from my twelve years of making KMA's outdoor installations, what an extraordinary (and entirely uncontrollable) impact the weather has on public art. For several weeks this autumn the plan was softened into something safer, and I stepped back from it; it is entirely to Viborg's credit, not mine, that they returned to the braver version. Only last week we were still arguing about whether the campfire animation needed pictures in it. We agreed, in the end, that the fire, the ring of light on people's faces and the voices ought to be enough, and I heard myself promise that if it turns out not to be, we will hold our hands up and say it was a mistake.

I meant it. But I notice that the nearer the opening gets, the more I want to add things, and I recognise the wish for what it is. A quiet festival has nowhere to hide. If it fails it will fail visibly, in front of people who trusted an Englishman with a theory, and who, unlike him, have to live there afterwards.

So I am anxious, and I would rather say so now than be wise after the event. But I still think it is the right way to go. Lars and I made a pact that night to sit in the same bar in five years' time and ask each other what on earth we did. I have no idea what the answer will be. In the meantime the cylinders are being lit, the benches are going in, and if you find yourself in Jutland this winter I would love to know what the angels asked you, and whether you stayed to answer.
